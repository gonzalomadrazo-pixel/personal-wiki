# Circle of Competence

*Topic group: Process and Temperament.* Investing only in what you can genuinely understand, and knowing where that boundary lies.

## What the sources say

- **[[Buffett 1987 Letter]]** (Warren Buffett): Buffett limits Berkshire's choices to investment alternatives it understands, and says that anyone who cannot value their business better than Mr. Market does not belong in the game.
- **[[Buffett 1993 Letter]]** (Warren Buffett): Buffett and Munger focus on sticking to easy cases because they cannot reliably distinguish between a fad and a durable franchise, which is a limitation of knowing what one cannot know.
- **[[Buffett 1996 Letter]]** (Warren Buffett): Buffett states that intelligent investing requires correctly evaluating selected businesses that are inside one's circle of competence, and knowing where that boundary lies is very important.
- **[[Marks 2006 Risk Memo]]** (Howard Marks): Marks argues that whether concentration or illiquidity is risky depends on the investor's knowledge: Buffett and Munger can hold concentrated, illiquid positions because they know what they are doing.
- **[[Munger 2016 Daily Journal Meeting]]** (Charlie Munger): Munger suggests avoiding topics outside one's circle of competence, noting that predicting winners in new tech areas is outside his scope, and that big long-term bets become less straightforward as one's firm grows.
- **[[Giverny 2002 Annual Letter]]** (Francois Rochon): Like Ted Williams swinging only at pitches in his best zones, Rochon waits for companies he can value and ignores sectors such as oil and gold regardless of price.
- **[[Nomad 2006 Annual Letter]]** (Nick Sleep): Sleep says treating an index as a risk-free home makes managers feel they must have an opinion on everything in it, regardless of their circle of competence.
