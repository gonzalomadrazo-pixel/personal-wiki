# Marks 2001 Cycles Memo — study notes

These are my notes on Howard Marks' November 2001 memo "You Can't Predict. You Can Prepare." Its main idea: you cannot forecast cycles, but you can know roughly where you stand in them and prepare.

## Knowing where you are vs. predicting (PDF p. 135)

- Marks dismisses economic forecasts as adding no value, but believes deeply in cycles and in preparing for them.
- This is not a contradiction. Knowing where we are in a cycle, and what that implies, is very different from predicting the timing, size and shape of the next move.

## General rules about cycles (PDF pp. 135-136)

- **Cycles are inevitable.** Every so often a trend runs long and people say "this time is different," pointing to new technology, institutions or behaviour. Then the old rules turn out to still apply. Few things go up forever or to zero.
- **Short financial memory makes cycles worse.** Investors forget the past, so each new generation treats old patterns as new discoveries (a point he takes from John Kenneth Galbraith).
- **Cycles correct themselves.** They don't need an outside shock. Trends create the causes of their own reversal: success contains the seeds of failure, and failure the seeds of success.
- **People see cycles as lopsided.** Falls are called "volatility" and panics, while rises are called "profit." Marks thinks market tops are also buying panics, with late capitulation by cautious investors.

## The economic cycle (PDF pp. 136-138)

- Many people forecast the economy; none has a much better record than the rest. Anyone who could reliably predict it would not give forecasts away for free.
- Markets already reflect the consensus forecast, so holding the consensus view can't earn above-average returns. A non-consensus view pays only if it is right, which is rare.
- Forecasts usually extrapolate current conditions. They are roughly right when nothing changes but miss the big surprises — where the big profits and losses are.
- The economist who calls a turn is usually an extremist who got lucky once, and is rarely right twice in a row.
- Examples of people declaring the business cycle dead: a 1996 newspaper article and 1928 quotes. The cycle always returned.
- How a non-forecaster copes: know where we are and lean against the wind. Buy cyclical companies when the economy is depressed and expansion has stopped; trim when demand is booming and the market forgets that peak earnings deserve trough multiples. Selling too early beats selling too late.

## The credit cycle (PDF pp. 138-140)

- Small changes in the economy cause large swings in the availability of credit, which move asset prices and feed back into the economy.
- The upswing: prosperity → capital providers grow → risk seems smaller → risk aversion fades → lenders compete by cutting rates, loosening standards, lending more and easing covenants → eventually unworthy projects get financed. "The worst loans are made at the best of times."
- The downswing: losses → lenders pull back → rates, restrictions and covenants rise → only the best borrowers get money → companies can't refinance → defaults, which deepen the contraction.
- At the bottom, little competition to lend means high returns are available with good credit quality, and contrarian lenders start the recovery.
- Loose credit fed many booms and busts (real estate 1989-92, emerging markets 1994-98, Long-Term Capital 1998, telecom and venture capital 2000-01, including the tech bubble). "Look around the next time there's a crisis; you'll probably find a lender."
- Marks worries more about the supply and demand for capital than about the economy. Investing where others are not throwing money is a big advantage.
- Staying power matters, and cash helps with it. His image: a six-foot man drowning in a stream that averages five feet deep. In late 2001 he expected cash to be king.

## The corporate life cycle, and business fads (PDF pp. 140-142)

- Companies move from frugal start-up, to growth and bureaucracy, to maturity and sometimes decline or bankruptcy. Bankruptcy can then restart the cycle with a leaner company.
- His biggest observed mistakes came when investors assumed ultra-high growth would last forever (1969 and 1999) and paid 50+ P/Es. Losses of 90% or more followed. Very few large companies sustain 15% annual earnings-per-share growth for decades; expect growth to revert toward the average.
- Business fashions swing like hemlines: diversification vs. specialisation (the conglomerate era), centralisation vs. decentralisation, lean inventories vs. buffers, high debt vs. equity cushions. There is no perfect answer, so companies swing back and forth.
- Views of technology-driven productivity in the late 1990s also proved cyclical.

## The market cycle and the pendulum (PDF pp. 142-145)

- Asset prices move far more than cash flows. Changes in valuation, driven mostly by investor psychology, explain most short-term price moves.
- Three stages of a bull market: (1) a few forward-looking people believe things will improve; (2) most investors see improvement happening; (3) everyone concludes things will improve forever.
- In the tech bubble, forced buying by index funds and benchmarked managers looked like a perpetual-motion machine, until prices collapsed under their own weight.
- The pendulum image (from his 1991 memo): market mood spends little time at the midpoint. It swings between euphoria and depression, between overpriced and underpriced, and the move toward an extreme powers the swing back.
- The warning signs of euphoria were visible even though the timing of the burst was not. But being too early looked like being wrong for years: some cautious investors lost clients or gave up and bought near the top.
- Conclusion: we may never know where we are going, but we had better have a good idea where we are.
