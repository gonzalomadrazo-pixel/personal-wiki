# Giverny 2002 Annual Letter — study notes

These are my notes on François Rochon's letter to Giverny Capital's clients (whom he calls partners) for 2002, the third year of the 2000-2002 bear market.

## Results in a bear market (PDF p. 14)

- In 2002 the Giverny portfolio returned -3%, versus -18% for its blended benchmark and -23% for the S&P 500 (all in Canadian dollars).
- From September 1993 to the end of 2002 it compounded at about 20% a year, versus about 10% for the benchmark and 11% for the S&P 500. Rochon notes a weakening Canadian dollar added roughly 2.7 points a year.
- The long-term goal is to beat the indexes by 5 percentage points a year. He warns it would be unrealistic to expect 20% a year to continue.
- He attributes the 2000-2002 market decline mainly to shrinking P/E ratios, especially among the largest companies.
- He invests his own money alongside clients. The number of partners grew from 8 in 1998 to more than 100 by the end of 2002.

## The Rule of Three (PDF p. 15)

- Rochon's **Rule of Three** lists three market realities to accept in advance:
  1. one year out of three, the stock market will fall by more than 10%;
  2. one stock out of three that he buys will disappoint;
  3. one year out of three, the portfolio will underperform the indexes.
- He calls it an observed, not scientific, rule. Its value is psychological: accepting bad years and stock mistakes beforehand helps you act rationally when they happen.
- The pattern is not regular. Bad years can bunch together. Sequoia Fund lagged the S&P 500 in its first four years (1970-73) before becoming a top performer. Keynes' Chest Fund lost more than the British market in its first three years, yet over 18 years earned about 9% a year versus about -1% for the market, while trailing in roughly one year out of three.
- Because the sequence can't be predicted, the best strategy is to stay invested and keep improving stock selection. He calls corrections "partners," since they create opportunities.

## Ted Williams and waiting for your pitch (PDF pp. 15-17)

- Baseball's Ted Williams divided the strike zone into 77 cells, measured his batting average in each, and swung only at pitches in his best zones. Rochon also cites Barry Bonds' patience at the plate.
- Investors have an even bigger advantage: there are no called strikes. They can let any number of pitches go by and wait for one that is easy to value. Rochon ignores sectors like oil and gold no matter what the price.

## The businesses owned (PDF pp. 17-19)

- He highlights holdings like M&T Bank (29% return on equity; he trusts CEO Robert Wilmers' judgment), Groupe BMTC (a debt-free furniture retailer whose EPS grew about 26% a year), Progressive, Cognex and Bed Bath & Beyond.
- About 70% of assets were in 10 stocks — a focused portfolio.
- He finally bought Johnson & Johnson in 2002 after regretting a missed purchase in 1994.

## Looking back: sales that went wrong, and things change (PDF pp. 19-20)

- Following J&J's habit of reviewing past acquisitions, he looks back at his own decisions. He cut Bombardier from about 20% of the portfolio in 1997; it rose 300% over the next three years and later collapsed in 2001-2002 amid recession and more debt.
- He sold Sun Microsystems in 1997; it rose another 1000% to a P/E near 100, then fell from about $60 to $3. His worry — competition from Intel's 64-bit chips — played out only years later.
- Lessons: it can take years to judge a decision, so be sceptical of a few good years. Investors look in the rear-view mirror more than through the windshield. Quoting Phil Fisher, it is easier to know what will happen than when. Few businesses stay great for decades, so keep watching the fundamentals.

## Owner's earnings as the scorecard (PDF pp. 20-21)

- Giverny measures itself by the growth in its companies' earnings and long-term prospects, not by short-term stock prices. This follows Buffett's look-through earnings idea.
- In 2002 the portfolio's stock prices were roughly flat, but its owner's earnings rose about 18-20%, with an ROE of about 18%. Over about five years, prices tend to reflect earnings growth.
- Gaps between price and business progress (Bed Bath & Beyond's EPS +40% with the stock +3%) are opportunities.

## Mistakes of the year: "Mistake du jour" (PDF pp. 21-23)

- **Bronze — Heartland Express:** a low-cost trucker with a founder-CEO owning 40%, a modest salary and no options. He sold in 1999 when growth paused; the stock tripled. The error was impatience.
- **Silver — Expedia:** he recommended it at $10 but hesitated to buy for all accounts, and waited in vain for the price to fall back. It later traded around $65. His lesson: discipline is following your rules; wisdom is knowing when to break them.
- **Gold — Richelieu Hardware:** a high-return, fast-growing distributor recommended by his partner Bernard Mooney. He waited years, bought too little and wouldn't pay a bit more. The stock rose about fivefold in five years — even though the lock on his own front door was made by Richelieu.

## The gardener and the investor (PDF pp. 23-24)

- Echoing Munger's advice to use many mental models, Rochon compares investing to Monet's garden at Giverny. Know your soil and climate (some sectors he can't understand). Tend the garden continuously. Don't pull the flowers to water the weeds, as Peter Lynch warned — that is, don't sell winners to buy more losers.
- Time and patience matter most. Great companies, like trees, take decades to grow. He likes long-tenured, founder-led companies such as Fastenal, Cognex, Bed Bath & Beyond and Costco.
