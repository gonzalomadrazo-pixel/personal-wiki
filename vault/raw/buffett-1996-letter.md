# Buffett 1996 Letter — study notes

These are my notes on the common-stock section of Buffett's letter for 1996: inactivity, "The Inevitables," circle of competence, advice for individual investors, and the USAir mistake.

## Inactivity as intelligent behaviour (PDF pp. 484-485)

- At the end of 1996 Berkshire's largest stocks were American Express, Coca-Cola, Disney, Freddie Mac, Gillette, McDonald's, The Washington Post and Wells Fargo, worth about $27.8 billion against roughly $7.9 billion of cost.
- Buffett jokes that Berkshire makes more money snoring than being active. Managers would never trade a wonderful subsidiary because of a rate forecast or a pundit's opinion, so why trade minority stakes in wonderful businesses?
- Buying public stocks is like buying subsidiaries: get a business with excellent economics and able, honest management at a sensible price, then just monitor whether those qualities last.
- Done well, this means a few holdings grow to dominate the portfolio. Selling them for that reason would be like a team trading its best player because he became too important.

## Preferring businesses unlikely to change (PDF pp. 485-486)

- Berkshire favours businesses and industries unlikely to face major change. It wants operations almost certain to be strong competitively in 10 or 20 years.
- Fast-changing industries may offer huge wins but not the certainty Buffett wants. As citizens he and Munger welcome innovation; as investors they applaud from the sidelines.
- See's Candies has changed its products, machines and channels. But why people buy boxed chocolates, and why they buy See's, is the same as in the 1920s and likely to stay so for decades.
- Coca-Cola's 1896 annual report already described the strategy it still follows a century later.

## The Inevitables and the Highly Probables (PDF pp. 486-487)

- Buffett calls companies like Coca-Cola and Gillette "The Inevitables." Forecasters may argue about exact volumes, but no honest observer doubts these companies will lead their fields worldwide for an investor's lifetime.
- High-tech or early-stage companies may grow faster in percentage terms, but he would rather be certain of a good result than hopeful of a great one.
- Real Inevitables are rare. Leadership alone guarantees nothing (GM, IBM and Sears once seemed invincible), and most leaders are "Impostors" open to attack. So Berkshire adds a few "Highly Probables."
- Two dangers remain even with great businesses: paying too much (a real risk in the overheated market of the time), and management losing focus by buying mediocre businesses. He notes Coke once grew shrimp and Gillette once explored for oil.

## Advice for individual investors; circle of competence (PDF pp. 487-488)

- Most investors, institutional or individual, will do best in a low-cost index fund. It will beat the net results of most professionals.
- For those who pick stocks: intelligent investing is not complex, but it is not easy. You must correctly evaluate *selected* businesses — only those inside your **circle of competence**. The size of the circle matters little; knowing where it ends matters a lot.
- You don't need beta, efficient-market theory, modern portfolio theory, option pricing or emerging markets. Buffett says an investing student needs just two good courses: how to value a business, and how to think about market prices.
- The goal: buy, at a rational price, part of an easy-to-understand business whose earnings will almost certainly be materially higher in 5, 10 and 20 years. Such companies are rare, so buy a meaningful amount when you find one.
- Stick to the rules. If you would not own a stock for ten years, don't own it for ten minutes. If a portfolio's combined earnings rise over the years, its market value will too — which is how Berkshire's look-through earnings and share price grew together.

## Mistake: the USAir preferred stock (PDF pp. 488-489)

- In 1989 Buffett invested $358 million in USAir 9.25% preferred stock. He calls his analysis superficial and wrong.
- He was swayed by the airline's long profit history and by the seeming safety of a senior security. He missed that its revenues now faced deregulated, fierce competition while its costs still reflected the protected, regulated era.
- The lesson: a good track record can mislead when an industry's economics have changed. History does not supply all the answers.
