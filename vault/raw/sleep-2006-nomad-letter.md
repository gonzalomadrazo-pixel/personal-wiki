# Nomad 2006 Annual Letter — study notes

These are my notes on the Nomad Investment Partnership's 2006 annual letter, written by Nick Sleep after he and Qais "Zak" Zakaria moved Nomad from Marathon to their own firm, Sleep, Zakaria and Company.

## Results and the index (PDF pp. 101-102)

- Nomad rose 13.6% in 2006 versus 20.1% for the MSCI World index, so an index fund would have done better that year.
- From its September 2001 start to the end of 2006, Nomad returned about 203% cumulatively before performance fees (about 23% a year), versus about 11% a year for the index.
- Sleep says the index is not their benchmark and that they spend no time on it. It appears in the letter only because of the firm's origins.
- A common mistake is treating the index as a risk-free "home." That leads to three errors: feeling you must have a view on everything in it (ignoring your circle of competence), passing up better opportunities, and over-diversifying.
- The index is just one opportunity set among many. It may beat Nomad in some years; what matters is the long-term destination. Their goal is a record comparable to investors they admire, such as Ruane, Tweedy, Klarman and Schloss.
- He gives two reasons for lagging in 2006. Portfolio companies were reinvesting heavily while the market rewarded high current free cash flow. And the setup of the new firm distracted them; they call non-investment distractions the biggest risk to their operation.

## The equity yield curve: patience has value (PDF pp. 103-104)

- Sleep's "equity yield curve": in bonds, longer maturities pay more to compensate for risk. In stocks, he argues, business outcomes can be *more* predictable several years out than next quarter, so time can both raise and de-risk returns.
- He suggests academic economics has a blind spot here because it studies short time periods, while habits and brands (like Coca-Cola's) take decades to form.
- Evidence from Nomad's own portfolio: stocks held longest had the highest annualised returns, falling steadily for newer holdings — a gap of more than 10 percentage points a year. He admits survivorship and timing biases.
- Other investors held Nomad's stocks for about 20 weeks on average, while Nomad expected to hold for about 260 weeks. Most investors trade on reported numbers; Nomad focuses on the inputs that drive future value.

## Scale economies shared: Amazon and Costco (PDF pp. 104-106)

- Amazon then earned roughly $500 million of free cash flow after heavy discretionary spending on growth. Sleep estimated that without that spending the business could return around $800 million a year. The market price implied investors thought the growth spending was worthless.
- Jeff Bezos explained that lowering prices always looks unprofitable in short-term elasticity math. The long-term judgment is that passing efficiency and scale savings back to customers creates a virtuous cycle and more free cash flow later. Free shipping and Prime were justified the same way.
- Sleep calls this the **scale economies shared** model. He analysed it earlier with Costco, and companies using it — Costco, Dell, Amazon and parts of Berkshire (GEICO, Nebraska Furniture Mart) — had come to dominate Nomad.
- Short-term traders don't disagree with Bezos' logic; they just don't think it will show up in the numbers soon. Nomad has "one big idea" while traders have many small ones. Judgment-based decisions are controversial until proven, but avoiding them limits innovation.
- An earlier Nomad appendix (PDF p. 65, "Measuring the Moat") proposed measuring moat strength by how a firm splits scale benefits between customers, employees and shareholders. It called this the "robustness ratio," using GEICO as the example.
- Portfolio at the time: the top five holdings were about half of assets and the top ten about three-quarters. The price-to-value ratio was in the upper 60 cents on the dollar.

## Mandates, takeovers and why problems go unsolved (PDF pp. 106-108)

- Many fund managers are boxed in by mandates (country, sector, style, listed vs. unlisted). Unconstrained investors should have an edge, which helps explain the private-equity boom.
- Sleep criticises UK take-private deals where insiders and private equity buy companies cheaply from passive institutional holders, who accept small premiums instead of defending long-term value. His example is Weetabix.
- His answer is to own enough of a company (more than 10% in the UK) to influence outcomes. Nomad became the largest holder of Games Workshop and joined a group of dissenting shareholders at Whitehead Mann.

## Fees designed to align incentives (PDF pp. 110-114)

- They will run only one fund and structured the firm so it cannot be sold to another manager.
- **Management fee**: a reimbursement of actual running costs, capped at 1% of assets and not meant to be a profit centre. As the fund grows, the fee falls as a percentage, so partners share in scale economies.
- **Performance fee**: 20% of gains above a 6% annual hurdle, paid into a reserve (the "Contingent Performance Reserve") instead of straight to the managers. The reserve refunds partners in years below the hurdle. The managers receive 20% of the running balance each year, so fees stay at risk for about five years, matching the holding period. They credit Orbis for the idea.
- They also gave themselves power to return cash to partners if the opportunity set shrinks, keeping the fund's size in line with its opportunities.
