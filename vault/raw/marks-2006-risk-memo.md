# Marks 2006 Risk Memo — study notes

These are my notes on Howard Marks' January 2006 memo devoted entirely to risk. A Deloitte article on corporate risk, read for a board meeting, prompted it.

## Why risk matters, and the misread risk-return line (PDF pp. 334-336)

- Capital-market theory: because people dislike risk, riskier investments must offer higher returns to attract money. Returns should be judged relative to the risk taken (risk-adjusted returns).
- The usual chart shows a line rising from low risk/low return to high risk/high return. Marks objects to the popular reading, "take more risk to make more money." If riskier investments reliably produced higher returns, they would not be riskier.
- The correct version: riskier investments must offer higher *expected* or *promised* returns, but nothing guarantees those returns will arrive.
- His redrawn chart shows, at each risk level, a range of possible outcomes that widens as risk rises. Higher risk brings a higher expected return, a chance of lower returns, and sometimes a chance of loss.

## Why volatility is not risk (PDF pp. 336-337)

- Academics chose volatility as the measure of risk mostly for convenience. It is objective, historical and easy to put in a formula.
- Marks has never met an investor who refused a deal because the price might fluctuate or have a down quarter. So volatility is probably not what the market prices.
- Practical flaws: a stock drifting from 50 to 80 and one falling from 50 to 20 can show the same volatility. A stock that rose smoothly to 80 and then dropped to 50 is called riskier at 50 than it was at 80. And historic volatility assumes the future will look like the past.

## What risk really is (PDF pp. 337-338)

- Risk is, first and foremost, the likelihood of losing money (permanent loss of capital) or of an unacceptably low return.
- Other risks are personal rather than built into the investment, so the market does not price them:
  - **falling short of a goal** — the same return can be fine for one investor and a shortfall for another;
  - **underperformance / benchmark risk** — anyone who deviates from an index will sometimes lag it badly (Buffett in 1999 is his example);
  - **career risk** — agents may avoid bets that could get them fired even if the bets are good for the client;
  - **unconventionality** — Keynes observed it is better for reputation to fail conventionally than succeed unconventionally;
  - **illiquidity** — being unable to turn an investment into cash when you need it.

## Measuring risk before and after the fact (PDF pp. 338-340)

- Looking forward, risk is an opinion. There is no standard way to put a number on it, and rare "improbable disasters" make investments look safer than they are (he quotes Nassim Taleb on Russian roulette with a huge revolver).
- Looking back, risk still can't be measured. Something happening does not mean it was likely; something not happening does not mean it was unlikely.
- Taleb's "alternative histories": was a winner skilful or lucky? A weather forecaster's 70% chance of rain can't be judged from one day. Judging risk-adjusted performance takes skilled, subjective judgment, not precise volatility ratios.

## Risk depends on the investor (PDF p. 341)

- Is concentration risky? Only for someone who cannot see the future clearly. Diversification is a trade of return for safety, made because our knowledge is imperfect.
- Is illiquidity risky? Buffett and Munger hold huge, hard-to-sell positions and whole companies without trouble, because they know what they are doing and won't face forced selling.
- Are distressed-debt funds risky? The holdings are messy, but the funds buy very cheaply, have skilled teams and a strong record. The answer depends on how you define risk. No single number captures risk, and few assets are risky or safe in themselves.

## Complexity and the limits of models (PDF pp. 342-343)

- Risk has several dimensions: unusual **scenarios**, **offsets** (true diversification means holdings that react differently to the same factor), **correlations** ("in a panic all correlations go to one") and **domino effects**. The 1998 emerging-markets/LTCM crisis spread through unexpected channels.
- He is sceptical of outside risk officers and models like Value at Risk. Models describe normal times, but the important events in financial history happen beyond two standard deviations. Investing rarely has the features — repetition, known rules, stable conditions, limited outcomes — that make risk suitable for modelling.

## Bearing risk intelligently for profit (PDF pp. 343-345)

- Keynes: a speculator knowingly runs risks; an investor runs risks without knowing it. Unknowingly bearing risk in popular, "can't-lose" assets is the big mistake. Knowingly accepting recognised risk at the right price is behind many of the best investments.
- Life-insurance analogy (his answer to a reporter about junk bonds): insurers know everyone will die, yet they are conservative businesses. The risk is known, can be analysed, can be diversified and is well paid for. Oaktree tries to do the same with high-yield bonds.
- Risky assets can be good investments if they are cheap enough; the skill is knowing when.
- Risk control is not risk avoidance. Avoiding all risk also means avoiding returns above the risk-free rate. The aim is "risk intelligence": understanding, managing and being paid for the risks you take.
