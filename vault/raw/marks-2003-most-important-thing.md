# Marks 2003 Most Important Thing — study notes

These are my notes on Howard Marks' July 2003 memo, which collects the principles Oaktree treats as "the most important thing." The memo lists eighteen points. I group them below.

## Price and value come first (PDF pp. 226-227)

- Above all, the relationship between price and value matters. No asset is so good it can't be a bad investment at too high a price, and few are so bad they can't be good investments if cheap enough.
- No asset class has a birthright to high returns. You'd ask the price before buying someone's car, so buying an investment without judging its price is just as silly. Examples: tech stocks in the late 1990s (bought at any price) and junk bonds in the 1970s-80s (avoided at any price).
- The biggest losses he has seen came from buying "perfect" companies at prices that assumed perfection. A good company is not the same as a good investment.
- He wants the best *buys*, not the best companies.

## A firmly held estimate of intrinsic value (PDF p. 227)

- Intelligent investing rests on rigorous estimates of intrinsic value, built from all available information and held with conviction.
- Without conviction, a price drop can shake an investor out (selling when they should buy more), and a price rise can tempt them to raise targets and buy more.
- Profitable positions are often unconventional and lonely, so a manager needs ego strength. But the conviction must be correct: few things are more dangerous than a wrong opinion held firmly and relied on too much.

## Invest defensively and avoid bad years (PDF pp. 227-229)

- Oaktree's route to success: avoid the losers, and the winners will take care of themselves.
- Defensive investing means fewer home runs but fewer strikeouts. Its ingredients: demand solid value at a bargain price, diversify rather than concentrate, and avoid relying on macro forecasts or market timing.
- He cites Buffett's margin of safety. Pay prices low enough that you can profit, or at least not lose, even if things go wrong.
- His shorthand is "invest scared": worry about loss, about what you don't know, and about bad luck. This prevents hubris and keeps portfolios prepared.
- Oaktree's goal is to be average in good times and far above average in bad times. Over time this should produce above-market returns with below-market volatility, and help clients avoid giving up at the bottom.

## The limits of macro forecasting; cycles; contrarianism (PDF pp. 229-231)

- Every investor must decide whether they belong to the "I know" school or the "I don't know" school. If you believe you can see the future, you concentrate and time the market. If not, you invest defensively and diversify. Marks is firmly in the second camp: survival comes first.
- Cycles are inevitable. Prices swing more than fundamentals, and extrapolating trends is one of the most dangerous things an investor can do.
- Contrarian behaviour works most reliably: only unpopular assets can be truly cheap. Distressed debt profits partly because no one loves a distressed company.
- Patient opportunism: Oaktree waits for sellers to call rather than chasing a buy list ("our investments find us"), and invests bottom-up.

## Running an investment firm (PDF pp. 231-233)

- Say what you will do, and then do it. Stay inside the client's mandate.
- Keep flexibility on tactics while being specific about philosophy, goals and style.
- Refuse to manage too much money. Good performance brings more assets, and too many assets eventually hurt performance. Oaktree has capped its strategies and turned money away for twenty years.

## Market efficiency, leverage and luck (PDF pp. 233-235)

- Mainstream large-cap stock markets are fairly efficient, so active fees rarely earn their keep there. Inefficient markets — uneven information, few or non-objective investors, specialised expertise needed — allow real mispricings. In those markets low risk and high return can go together. Marks chose to work only in inefficient markets.
- Be wary of leverage. It raises potential return but also raises risk and removes the margin of safety. It is acceptable to exploit unusually generous opportunities, but dangerous for squeezing big returns out of thin margins.
- Much of short-term performance is out of anyone's control: competitors, the environment and luck. Something probable may not happen, and something that happened may have been improbable. Judging managers therefore can't be purely quantitative.

## Communication, people and principles (PDF pp. 235-238)

- Tell it like it is: be candid, don't take credit for things that went right for the wrong reasons, and admit mistakes without excuses.
- Keep staff turnover low by hiring team players, keeping a collegial culture and sharing ownership. The goal is to own less and less of a firm that becomes worth more and more.
- Partnerships work with shared values, complementary skills, mutual respect and people you enjoy working with.
- Have a mission beyond assets under management. Consistent, principled answers matter more than any single answer. His final, nineteenth point: never think it will be easy.
