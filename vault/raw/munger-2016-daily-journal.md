# Munger 2016 Daily Journal Meeting — study notes

These are my notes on the investing and decision-making parts of Charlie Munger's Q&A at the 2016 Daily Journal annual meeting. It is an informal, transcribed conversation, not a written letter. I skipped his political comments.

## Daily Journal's business shift (PDF pp. 669-671)

- The newspaper once had near-monopoly economics but was shrinking with the rest of the industry, down to roughly $1 million a year of pre-tax profit.
- During the foreclosure boom it handled most foreclosure notices. Munger compared this to being an undertaker in a plague year. It used that cash to buy stocks cheaply during the panic.
- It then moved into court and public-agency software. He called it slow, expensive and painful, but potentially very "sticky" once entrenched, and said shareholders now owned something like a venture-capital operation.

## Circle of competence and the IBM question (PDF pp. 671-672)

- Asked whether Buffett's IBM purchase fell outside his circle of competence, Munger said the outcome was a mystery to him. IBM's old business was sticky and would decline slowly, but whether its new product would succeed was unclear.
- At Berkshire's size, the big long-term bets are no longer as easy as they once were.
- On private unicorns such as Uber and Airbnb, he said predicting which new tech companies win is outside his circle of competence, so he avoids the topic (p. 690).

## Few big decisions, patience, then aggression (PDF pp. 672-673)

- He described a 1962 oil-royalty auction where only low-quality bidders participated. A small investment paid his family about $100,000 a year for decades. The catch: stories like this happen only once in a while.
- Blue Chip Stamps and Wesco became hugely successful largely because of five or six transactions over a long time, not many small acquisitions.
- You make money by waiting — patience, followed by aggressive action when the chance comes. Daily Journal happened to catch the bottom by luck, but having cash ready when others were running away was not luck.

## Opportunity cost and valuation (PDF pp. 675-676)

- On discount rates: both the government-bond rate and your own opportunity cost matter. The risk-free rate affects the value of all assets, but your next-best alternative should drive your own decisions.
- Most people pay too little attention to opportunity cost; bridge and poker players understand it.
- Valuation is not a formula. It weighs many factors at once, like a bridge hand. If a formula made people rich, every math whiz would be rich. Different businesses get different treatment, and Berkshire will pay more for a good business than a lousy one.

## Multiple mental models and synthesis (PDF pp. 676-679)

- Using multiple models means thinking about many things at once. Investing is never easy, and anyone who finds it easy is fooling themselves.
- He and Buffett keep uncrowded schedules and spend a lot of time reading and thinking, sifting for a few opportunities and seizing some. He criticises multitasking as bad for the quality of thinking.
- Specialisation is the right career advice for most people. But specialising alone leads to terrible mistakes elsewhere, so broad synthesis across disciplines is a second, defensive tool.

## Rationality and avoiding self-destruction (PDF pp. 679, 681)

- Being rational largely means avoiding places where the usual result is awful: anger, resentment, envy, jealousy and self-pity.
- Old-fashioned virtues — reliability, honesty, discipline and a bit of generosity — make life simpler and work better.

## Judging management and industries (PDF pp. 678, 686, 689, 692-693)

- Wells Fargo: Berkshire bought during banking panics because it believed Wells' lending officers were unusually shrewd and careful, an information advantage from general knowledge. Banking is dangerous for investors without deep insight, because banks can easily report earnings they aren't really earning.
- Autos: fiercely competitive and commoditised. You'd need a clearly superior competitor.
- Commodities such as oil and iron ore swing to extreme highs and lows. He has never profited from predicting them; Berkshire buys good businesses and rides out the bumps.
- Bank of America was bought the classic way: it had fallen far below its value.
- Coca-Cola: full-sugar Coke volume was now declining, but the company's broader distribution system kept it a solid, if no longer easy, investment.

## Incentives and finance culture (PDF pp. 683-685, 690-691)

- He argues large amounts of easy money cause regrettable behaviour, which he called "Munger's rule." His example: venture rounds that quietly subordinate earlier investors.
- He sees too much of finance as a legalised gambling culture, and says being shrewd at buying pieces of paper is not an adequate life on its own.
- Fundamental value investing will never be obsolete: to succeed you must buy things for less than they are worth.
