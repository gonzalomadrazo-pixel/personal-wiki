# Buffett 1987 Letter — study notes

These are my notes on the investing sections of Buffett's letter for 1987. They paraphrase the letter; they are not a copy of it.

## Business value vs. book value (PDF pp. 215-217)

- Berkshire's net worth grew 19.5% in 1987. Book value per share had compounded at about 23% a year over the 23 years of current management.
- Buffett stresses that what matters is growth in per-share *business* value, not book value. The two can be unrelated. He gives examples of companies that reported healthy book values shortly before bankruptcy, and of Belridge Oil, which sold for far more than its book value.
- He praises seven large non-financial subsidiaries (Buffalo News, Fechheimer, Kirby, Nebraska Furniture Mart, Scott Fetzer, See's, World Book). Together they earned roughly a 57% after-tax return on the equity capital they used, with almost no debt. His inferences: their business value is far above book value, they need little capital to grow, and they are run by exceptional managers.
- He and Munger leave operations to the managers. Their main contribution is informed applause.

## Buying stocks as if buying a private business (PDF p. 230)

- When Berkshire buys common stocks, it evaluates them as if buying part of a private business: the economic prospects, the people running it, and the price.
- It has no planned sale time or price. It will hold as long as intrinsic value keeps growing at a satisfactory rate.
- Buffett calls himself a business analyst — not a market analyst, a macroeconomic analyst or a security analyst.
- An active market is useful because it sometimes offers great prices, but it is not necessary. A long suspension of trading would not bother him.

## Mr. Market as a business partner (PDF pp. 230-231)

- He retells Ben Graham's allegory. Imagine market quotes coming from an emotional partner, Mr. Market, who offers every day to buy your share of a business or sell you his.
- Mr. Market's mood swings between euphoria (very high prices) and depression (very low prices), even when the underlying business is stable.
- You are free to ignore him; he will return tomorrow with a new price. The more erratic his moods, the better for the investor.
- The key warning: Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you. His prices are useful; his opinions are not. If you cannot value your business better than he can, you should not be playing.
- Buffett says success comes from combining good business judgment with the ability to keep your thinking insulated from market emotion, not from formulas, computer models or price signals.
- He judges holdings by their operating results, not by daily or yearly quotes. He repeats Graham's idea that in the short run the market is a voting machine and in the long run a weighing machine. Slow recognition by the market can even help, because it allows buying more at bargain prices.

## When to sell, and the three permanent holdings (PDF pp. 232-233)

- Berkshire sells when the market clearly overvalues a business, or when it needs money for something more undervalued or better understood. It does not sell just because a stock has risen or has been held a long time.
- Three stocks — Capital Cities/ABC, GEICO and The Washington Post — are treated as permanent holdings, like wholly owned subsidiaries. He would not sell them even if they became overpriced (except, if absolutely necessary, to pay extraordinary insurance losses).
- He prefers a wonderful business at a sensible price to a mediocre business at a bargain price. He jokes that it took him about 20 years to learn this, during which his "bargains" included a farm-equipment maker, a department store and a textile mill.

## Controlled companies vs. marketable stocks; capital allocation (PDF pp. 233-235)

- Owning a controlled company has two advantages: Berkshire gets to allocate its capital, and it has tax advantages at 80%+ ownership.
- Many CEOs reach the top through marketing, production or administration and are not skilled at capital allocation. Yet after ten years, a CEO whose company retains 10% of net worth a year has deployed more than 60% of all its capital.
- The stock market sometimes compensates by offering pieces of great businesses at far below negotiated-deal prices. Examples: Washington Post shares bought in 1973 and GEICO shares bought in 1976-80 at prices below their 1987 annual per-share earnings.
- Accounting quirk: GAAP shows the market value of these minority stakes on the balance sheet, but only their small dividends in income. Buffett ignores GAAP and focuses on future earning power.

## Other securities, portfolio insurance and the 1987 crash (PDF pp. 235-237)

- Berkshire's insurance float can go into five categories: long-term stocks, medium-term bonds, long-term bonds, cash equivalents and arbitrage. It picks whichever offers the best after-tax "mathematical expectation," but only among things it understands.
- He blames 1987's volatility on big money managers who focused on what other managers would do next, rather than on the businesses.
- He criticises "portfolio insurance," which mechanically sells more as prices fall — like selling pieces of a farm whenever a neighbour's land sells cheaper.
- His conclusion: volatile markets are good for real investors, small or large. Volatility can only hurt an investor who is forced, financially or psychologically, to sell at bad times.
- Berkshire found little to buy in stocks in 1987 but expected Mr. Market to offer chances later.

## Bonds, arbitrage and Salomon (PDF pp. 237-240)

- He was wary of long-term bonds because of inflation risk tied to U.S. trade deficits and government borrowing. He held medium-term tax-exempt bonds as a parking place.
- Arbitrage: a few large, publicly announced deals a year. He estimates at least 25% average annual pre-tax returns historically.
- Berkshire bought $700 million of Salomon 9% convertible preferred stock. The main reason was trust in CEO John Gutfreund, not any insight into investment-banking economics.
